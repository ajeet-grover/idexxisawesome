# Competitive Matrix — Streak & Micro-Learning Apps

> Context: Streakly's Day-7 retention dropped from 48% to 39%; users break their streak in week 1 and mostly don't return. This scan looks at how comparable apps handle streaks, daily habits, and post-lapse re-engagement — to find white space for the Comeback experience. Researched via web search, 2026-09-28.

## Scope

Selected 5 apps built around streaks, daily habits, or micro-learning (excludes social media and long-form course platforms): **Duolingo, Babbel, Elevate, Brilliant, and Streaks** (Crunchy Bagel).

## Competitor Profiles

### Duolingo
- **Core features:** Gamified, bite-sized daily lessons (language, plus expanding into math, music, chess); XP, leagues/leaderboards; AI-powered conversation practice (Video Call), now expanding from its Max tier to Super subscribers.
- **Pricing model:** Free (ad-supported, limited by an "Energy" system); a new lower-cost "Lite" tier (~$35.99/yr, still has ads); Super Duolingo (~$12.99/mo or ~$95.99/yr, removes ads and Energy limits); Family plan (~$119.99/yr for 6); Duolingo Max (~$30/mo AI tier, being phased out for new subscribers).
- **Target customer:** Mass-market casual learners; broad age range; users receptive to game mechanics.
- **Engagement after week 1:** Streak counter is the centerpiece. Streak freezes are purchasable with in-app currency or earned at milestones (100-day streak grants 3 free freezes), capped at 2 active at a time — freezes must be held *before* a miss, not applied after. Backed by frequent mascot-driven push notifications and leaderboards.
- **Notable recent changes:** Replaced the older "hearts" system with a more restrictive "Energy" system, which triggered visible user backlash — rage-quitting and uninstalls cited as dragging down 2025 DAU growth. Monetization is shifting from ads toward AI features/avatars/in-app purchases rather than more ads.

### Babbel
- **Core features:** Structured, linguist-built courses; video lessons with native speakers; dialogues and comprehension checks; supplementary podcasts and games; adding AI-powered dialogue practice and more personalized paths.
- **Pricing model:** Subscription only — ~$14.99/mo month-to-month for one language, with discounted multi-month/annual plans (roughly $8–15/mo effective). No ad-supported free tier comparable to Duolingo's.
- **Target customer:** Adults who want a structured, "serious" path to conversational fluency — explicitly positions itself against Duolingo's gamified style.
- **Engagement after week 1:** Has a basic streak counter but deliberately avoids heavy gamification — no mascot pressure, treasure chests, or XP grind. Leans on content and structure, not loss-aversion mechanics, to bring people back.
- **Notable recent changes:** Being cited as a landing spot for users leaving Duolingo after the Energy backlash; investing in AI-powered, more personalized dialogue practice.

### Elevate
- **Core features:** 40+ short games across reading, writing, speaking, math, and memory; adaptive difficulty; a "study area" with skill tips; 150+ achievements and a workout calendar.
- **Pricing model:** Free tier limited to ~3 games/day with no skill selection; Premium ~$39.99/yr (~$4/mo effective) or $9.99/mo, with a 7-day free trial.
- **Target customer:** Adults/professionals focused on cognitive fitness and workplace-adjacent skills, rather than a single subject like language.
- **Engagement after week 1:** Streak counter with an *automatic* streak freeze — every member gets 2 free freezes at account creation, can earn more at milestones, or buy 1–2 more. Customizable daily reminder notifications.
- **Notable recent changes:** No major public gamification overhaul found; its freeze mechanic (auto-granted, milestone-replenished) is one of the more forgiving default setups in this set, but it's still a proactive-protection tool, not a post-break recovery flow.

### Brilliant
- **Core features:** Interactive, hands-on courses in math, CS, data science, and logic; daily challenges; competitive leagues; "Koji," an adaptive AI tutor that tracks mastery and targets gaps.
- **Pricing model:** Free tier with daily challenges, a Math & Science Wiki, and limited lesson unlocks via a "key" system; Premium ~$27.99/mo or ~$161.88/yr, 7-day free trial.
- **Target customer:** STEM-minded learners — students and professionals building math/CS/quantitative skills; more technical/academic than Duolingo's mass-market base.
- **Engagement after week 1:** Daily challenges and streak tracking paired with competitive leagues; short daily goals designed to be easy to keep up "even on busy days."
- **Notable recent changes:** Introduction of Koji, an adaptive AI tutor — the most content-personalization-forward feature in this set, though it's about what to learn next, not about recovering from a broken streak.

### Streaks (Crunchy Bagel)
- **Core features:** General-purpose habit tracker, not tied to learning content — up to 24 customizable daily tasks, timed activities, negative-habit tracking, flexible scheduling, automatic completion for health-linked tasks, home-screen/Apple Watch widgets, Live Activities.
- **Pricing model:** One-time purchase (~$5.99), no subscription, no in-app purchases — a distinctly different model from every other app here.
- **Target customer:** Apple-ecosystem power users who already know what habits they want to build and want a lightweight tracker rather than guided content.
- **Engagement after week 1:** Pure streak-and-widget mechanics, no content to pull people back — relies on smart reminders and frictionless logging (automatic completion via HealthKit) to prevent a miss in the first place, not on recovering after one.
- **Notable recent changes:** Platform-support investment (iOS 18 Control Center widgets, watchOS 11 double-tap gestures, Live Activities) — friction-reduction to log a habit, not forgiveness mechanics after a break.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Duolingo** | Gamified language (+ new subjects) | Free (ads/Energy) → Super ~$13/mo → Max ~$30/mo (phasing out) | Mass-market casual learners | Purchasable/earned freezes, capped at 2, must be held *before* a miss | New "Energy" system caused user backlash and uninstalls |
| **Babbel** | Structured language courses | Subscription only, ~$15/mo (discounted multi-month/annual) | Adults wanting serious fluency, gamification-averse | Basic streak, deliberately low-pressure — no forgiveness mechanic needed because there's little punishment | Gaining ex-Duolingo users; adding AI dialogue practice |
| **Elevate** | Cognitive/professional skill games | Free (limited) → Premium ~$4–10/mo | Professionals building cognitive fitness | 2 free freezes at signup, more via milestones/purchase — proactive protection only | Stable; no major recovery-flow changes found |
| **Brilliant** | STEM/quant micro-lessons | Free (limited) → Premium ~$28/mo or ~$162/yr | STEM-minded students/professionals | Daily challenges + leagues; no freeze/recovery flow found | Koji adaptive AI tutor (personalizes *what's next*, not recovery) |

## White Space for Streakly

1. **No one offers a real post-break comeback experience — only pre-break protection.** Every freeze mechanic here (Duolingo, Elevate) has to be held or earned *before* the miss happens; once a streak is actually broken, all five apps just show the reset (or, in Babbel's case, never made a big deal of it in the first place). None of them acknowledge the break, show what the user already built, or offer an active way to rebuild momentum in that moment. This is exactly the gap Streakly's own user interviews and NPS feedback point to, and it's unclaimed across this whole set.
2. **No one personalizes the response to *why* a streak broke.** Every mechanic treats a missed day identically — either you had a freeze banked, or you didn't. There's no distinction between a legitimate life disruption (travel, illness) and genuine disengagement, and no reason-specific response to bring someone back. Streakly's working hypothesis — a comeback reason specific to the user's own progress, not a generic nudge — has no direct competitor equivalent in this set.

Duolingo's Energy backlash is a useful data point for both: making the punitive side of the mechanic *harsher* visibly drove uninstalls and pushed users toward the comparatively gentler Babbel — evidence that moving in the opposite direction (softer, recovery-oriented) is a real, demonstrated market opportunity, not just an internal hypothesis.
